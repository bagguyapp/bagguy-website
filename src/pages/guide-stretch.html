Budgeting

How to stretch your food dollars all month

Last reviewed October 23, 2026 · 7 minute read · Sources listed below

The hardest part of a monthly food budget is often the last week. These habits, drawn from USDA's MyPlate "healthy eating on a budget" advice, help your groceries last from one deposit to the next.

1. Plan around your deposit date

SNAP benefits arrive once a month on a schedule set by your state. Instead of one big trip, divide your month into four weekly amounts.

  • Week 1: stock up on shelf-stable staples and freezer items while you have the most room in your budget: rice, oats, dry or canned beans, pasta, canned fish, frozen vegetables.
  • Weeks 2 to 4: use smaller trips for fresh produce, milk, eggs, and bread, so less goes to waste.
  • Keep a small reserve for the last few days so you aren't caught short before your next deposit.
Example only: If your household has $240 for the month, that's about $60 a week. This is an illustration, not a recommendation. Your amount and needs will differ.

2. Make a list, and shop from what you have first

MyPlate's advice for shopping on a budget comes down to plan, compare, and prepare. Before you shop, check your fridge, freezer, and pantry, then plan meals that use what you already have. Build your list around those meals and stick to it. Planned meals mean fewer impulse buys and less food in the trash.

3. Compare unit prices, not sticker prices

The shelf tag usually shows a unit price, such as the price per ounce or per pound, in small print. It's the fairest way to compare different sizes and brands.

  • Bigger packages are often cheaper per ounce, but not always. Check the tag.
  • Store brands are often cheaper than name brands for the same kind of product.
  • Only buy in bulk what you'll actually use or can freeze before it goes bad.

4. Lean on low-cost, healthy staples

These foods tend to give you a lot of nutrition for the money:

FoodWhy it stretches your budget
Dry or canned beans and lentilsProtein and fiber for a low cost per serving. Rinse canned beans to cut sodium.
EggsA flexible protein for any meal.
Frozen fruits and vegetablesJust as nutritious as fresh, and they last for months without waste.
Canned fish and canned vegetablesShelf-stable. Look for "low sodium" or "packed in water."
Oats, brown rice, and whole-grain pastaFilling whole grains that store well.
Seasonal produceFruits and vegetables in season are often cheaper and taste better.
Family-size packs of meatOften cheaper per pound. Split them into portions and freeze.

5. Cook once, eat twice

Make a big batch of soup, chili, or a casserole and freeze portions for busy nights. Turn leftovers into new meals: roast chicken becomes tacos, extra rice becomes fried rice.

6. Use deals and coupons, but only for things on your list

Weekly ads and digital coupons help most when they line up with what you already planned to buy. A "deal" on something you don't need isn't saving. Bag Guy's SNAP-friendly filter is designed to help with this: save the offers that match your list, then scan your receipt after you shop.

7. Look into extra help where you live

Some states and communities run programs that add extra value when you buy fruits and vegetables with SNAP, often at farmers markets or participating stores. Ask your state SNAP agency or local food bank what's available near you. For school-age children, ask your school about free or reduced-price meals.
